\section{Introduction}

I grew up in Beijing since childhood and witnessed firsthand how the air quality of this city changed from frequent smog in winter to a significantly cleaner sky. This improvement made me believe that the clean heating policy was successful and necessary. However, recent public discussions, as well as my field research in a rural city in Hebei Province, have led me to face a more challenging issue: Part of the improvement in urban environment might come at the cost of rural families in North China having to bear higher winter heating costs and facing new transformation risks.

Winter heating is a basic livelihood need in rural areas of North China. Since the mid-2010s, when the country promoted clean heating, tens of millions of farmers have shifted from burning coal to using gas, electricity, or heat pumps as part of the ``coal-to-X'' transformation \cite{li2025scatteredcoal,ndrc2017plan}. Regional air quality has improved, and winter coal consumption has significantly decreased. However, the environmental benefits do not automatically translate into sustainable energy use for every household. During a rural survey in Hebei Province, I found that even within the same village, each household varies in terms of income, housing area, savings, heating habits, equipment conditions, and risk tolerance. What is affordable for one household may become a heavy burden for another.

The specificity of the policy benefits lies in the fact that China's clean heating initiatives are usually organized and implemented at the village and town levels rather than being decided on a household-by-household basis. Upper-level decision-makers often lack detailed data on the optimal heating path for each household, so projects that are technically ``completed'' may still deviate from the daily needs of the people. During the initial stage of renovation, the investment and the regular winter bills are mainly borne by the households; as the equipment subsidies and operation subsidies gradually decrease in batches, many households find themselves in a familiar dilemma: they can afford to install the equipment, but they cannot afford to use it. The proportion of heating expenses in the annual income has increased, the indoor temperature has dropped, and some households have returned to burning coal or maintaining dual fuel usage.

The disparity between environmental benefits and household affordability has drawn attention from both domestic and international media; media reports have also made more people aware of the difficulties of rural clean heating. However, at the same time, many observers still do not understand the underlying mechanism, and some online discussions are easily influenced by one-sided or false information. Existing research has documented this tension from multiple perspectives: Environmental and health studies show that clean heating can reduce PM\textsubscript{2.5} exposure, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon pollution and carbon emissions, but the benefits and costs are not evenly distributed among households \cite{liu2025pahs,yuan2025carbon,zhang2024uneven}; Energy economy and energy poverty studies have found that if gas expenses exceed about 5\% of disposable income, it will significantly change behavior, and the withdrawal of subsidies will increase winter costs and expand the proportion of households facing affordability pressure \cite{zhao2024sustainable,liu2020costs,zhai2023review}; Field evidence has also reported county-level subsidy differences, electricity/gas price pass-through, equipment replacement cycles and coal rebound \cite{hu2021decoalizing}. Regional policy and household choice research links household actions with results at the village and city levels, but tools that allow users to input household income, housing area and heating costs, interactively compare natural gas, heat pumps and insulation paths, and experience subsidy withdrawal and compliance pressure in a single session are still rare.

This study was specifically designed to address this gap, and it developed, deployed, and evaluated a \textbf{village-level clean heating transformation simulation sandbox} (\url{https://www.clean-heating-simulator.com}). Participants entered the scenario as farmers, entered their family income, savings, housing area, and pre-transition heating costs, and then made choices in five rounds---whether to transform, which technology to adopt, how to increase income or save energy, how to deal with the withdrawal of subsidies---while viewing real-time indicators such as heating costs, energy burden rate, legal compliance, and emission scores. The AI in this case is not a decorative chatbot that appears at the end of the game; it reads user input, step-by-step choices, and final outcomes, and translates these data trajectories into farmer profiles, including income pressure, housing size, cost affordability, compliance risks, and vulnerability to subsidy reduction; based on this, it explains which heating transformation path is more appropriate and why, enabling discussions on low-carbon emission reduction, economic affordability, and livelihood security. The simulation engine itself is deterministic and calibrated based on literature and public statistics; the AI adds personalized explanations and suggestions, without changing the scoring rules.

The research question is: \textit{Can the household-level simulation supported by AI help users of different identities---students, farmers who have undergone transformation and those who have not, and other stakeholders---understand the trade-offs between environmental goals, affordability of heating, and compliance, and provide household-level evidence and personalized path recommendations?} We assume: (H1) Farmer users can identify the coal-to-X transformation path that better suits their own household conditions; (H2) Students and other non-farmer users will have an improved understanding of the dynamic aspects of heating costs after one experience. From June 28th to July 5th, 2026, we recruited 21 complete case studies (including rural users in the administrative regions of Hebei Province and Beijing) through course mobilization and public links, using pre- and post-test questionnaires, and storing behavioral logs in Supabase.

The entire paper is structured as follows: The literature review summarizes the evidence in the areas of environmental benefits, affordability pressure, technological choices, and policy simulations; The methods section explains the deployed products, designs, informed consents, and analysis plans; The results section reports the data from questionnaires, logs, and simulation outcomes; The discussion section interprets how these results address the research questions and H1/H2, and points out the limitations; The conclusion section summarizes the contributions, implications, and future directions.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Environmental benefits of clean heating}

Previous studies have first established the environmental significance of ``coal-to-X'' heating. The national clean heating policy aims to replace rural coal usage and defines it as a key path to reduce winter air pollution in the north and increase the coverage of clean heating \cite{ndrc2017plan,li2025scatteredcoal}. Empirical research on rural clean heating also shows that replacing coal can reduce PM\textsubscript{2.5}, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH), and carbon emission pressure \cite{liu2025pahs,yuan2025carbon}; however, the distribution of health benefits and economic costs varies among different households \cite{zhang2024uneven}. Therefore, clean heating is not only a technical replacement at the household level, but also a component of air pollution control, carbon reduction, and rural energy transition.

\subsection{Affordability, energy poverty, and coal rebound}

The second type of research indicates that environmental benefits are often accompanied by cost pressures at the household level. A review of the comprehensive renovation of clean heating in northern rural areas identified high operating costs, weak infrastructure, and ``coal rebound'' as the main obstacles \cite{zhai2023review}. The research by Liu and Mauzerall on the cost of clean heating showed that in the absence of subsidies, the gas and electricity paths could be much more expensive than burning coal \cite{liu2020costs}. Wang et al. further discovered that the withdrawal of subsidies would increase winter heating expenses and expand the proportion of households facing affordability pressure \cite{zhao2024sustainable}. Hu's research on the ``2+26'' region also recorded coal rebound and reliance on subsidies \cite{hu2021decoalizing}. In summary, these studies indicate that transformation cannot be considered sustainable merely because equipment has been installed; households must also be able to afford daily winter use.

\subsection{Technology routes and village-level choice}

The third type of research focuses on the differences among various ``coal-to-X'' transformation paths. Gas, electric heating, air-source heat pumps, ground-source heat pumps, and insulation renovations vary in terms of initial investment, operating costs, comfort level, reliance on infrastructure, and maintenance. Existing cost and policy studies indicate that clean heating renovations should be matched with local resources, household income, building conditions, and operating costs \cite{liu2020costs,zhao2024sustainable,zhai2023review,qin2026heating}. Since many rural heating transitions are organized and implemented at the village and town levels, if a single path does not suit the majority of households, a gap will arise between ``policy completion'' and ``family affordability''.

\subsection{Policy simulation, AI methods, and this study's gap}

The fourth type of literature provides a basis for the methods adopted in this study. Based on the agent-based and multi-agent simulation methods, individual behaviors can be linked to the results of group or regional policies \cite{wang2023abm,ma2024agi}. Both policy studies and research on family choices have shown how individual heating decisions interact with regional clean heating outcomes \cite{hu2021decoalizing,zhang2024uneven}. Existing research can demonstrate environmental benefits, identify affordable risks, and compare technical paths. However, there are still few tools that are accessible to the public, allowing farmers and other users to input household income, housing area, and heating costs, and compare natural gas, heat pumps, insulation, subsidy reduction, and compliance pressure in the same interface. This study fills this gap by converting regional policy issues into village-level decision-making sandboxes at the household level, in a round-robin format, with recorded data.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Artifact}

We have developed a \textbf{village-level clean heating transformation simulation sandbox} for the ``coal-to-X'' scenario in rural areas of North China (\url{https://www.clean-heating-simulator.com}; hosted as a single-page \texttt{index.html} on Vercel). Participants do not need to create an account. On the welcome page, choose your identity (student, transformed farmer, untransformed farmer, or other stakeholder), and fill in basic background information, such as total annual income of the family, annual savings, housing area, permanent population, and winter heating costs before the transformation. The roles (farmer), regions (North China), and initial heating method (unprocessed coal) remain constant.

There are a total of five rounds of simulation: (1) Current situation selection (continue burning coal or prepare for transformation); (2) Compliance pressure (if continuing to burn coal, a probability-based household inspection will be conducted based on the weighted sum of housing area and the number of consecutive non-compliance occurrences); (3) Technical path (natural gas, ground source heat pump or air source heat pump; the renovation and operation costs are scaled linearly based on a 100 m\textsuperscript{2} benchmark); (4) Increase in income or energy conservation (choose one from the three options); (5) Subsidy reduction (the initial coal expenditure is converted to equivalent gas volume, and then a fixed increase in the cost per cubic meter is added, followed by the final determination). The interface displays six indicators in real time---annual heating cost, income, surplus, legal compliance (0--100), emission score (0--100), energy burden rate (\%)---and the narrative log for each round. The final result is: successful transformation, financial strain, or law enforcement-related seizure.

After the final stage, participants can request the \textbf{AI replay}: The browser will POST the initial input, the step-by-step log and the final state to \texttt{/api/chat}; the server will call the \textbf{DeepSeek Chat Completions API} (\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}), along with one of the three manually-written structured system prompts (comparison for transformed farmers / suggestions for untransformed farmers / default analysis). The model must only use the numerical values in the log and return three Markdown sections. If the API key is missing or the call fails, the interface will display an error---\textbf{no fallback will be used with the pre-written simulation script}. The simulation engine itself is deterministic; the emission factors are anchored to the official power emission factor data and village building carbon standards \cite{mee2011ghg,mee2020grid,cabr2025rural}, while the economic, compliance and policy impact constants are calibrated based on literature, public statistics, and media/interview reports (\texttt{algori\_spec.md}, \texttt{research/data/calibration\_defaults.json}).

Each conversation is stored in Supabase's \texttt{simulation\_sessions} with an anonymous UUID, including baseline input, round selection ID/tag, \texttt{event\_log}, final type/summary, final metrics, AI analysis text (if generated), and post-test questionnaire fields.

\subsection{Design}

We adopted a \textbf{single-exposure design}, combining pre-test and post-test questionnaires with behavioral logs. The intervention consisted of completing a full run of the sand table (with the option of including AI replay and post-test questionnaires).

\textbf{Independent variables (IVs):} (a) Exposure to the online sandbox, along with self-selected paths (technical, compliance branches, revenue-increase/energy-saving options---not experimenter-assigned); (b) Participant identity (student, transformed farmer, untransformed farmer, others) as a between-group factor.

\textbf{Dependent variables (DVs):} (1) \textbf{Questionnaire cognition/attitude}---for students and other non-farmers: pre-test Q1 and post-test Q2 (a 5-point Likert scale for understanding of coal-to-gas conversion and heating costs), Q4 (whether changing strategies would be different if replayed), S1 (students' evaluation of the simulator's usefulness), O2 (other audiences' understanding of family differences), willingness to recommend, and whether AI is helpful; for already transformed farmers: current heating method, post-transition cost range, cost-saving behavior, cost realism (F4), most matching/most mismatching stage (F6--F7), and subsidy response; for untransformed farmers: current heating method, concerns (N2), attitude change (N3), preference path (N4). (2) \textbf{Behavioral logs}---five rounds of choices, whether entering the technology path selection, choice of natural gas/ground source heat pump/air source heat pump, and final type. (3) \textbf{Simulation results}---final energy burden, heating cost, surplus, compliance, emission score, and tonnage of carbon dioxide.

\textbf{Pre-test and post-test structure:} Non-farm households completed the \textbf{single-item pre-test} (Q1) before the simulation, and an approximately \textbf{2-minute post-test} after the AI replay. \textbf{Transformed and untransformed farmers skipped the pre-test} and only completed the post-test. The latest post-test mainly consists of radio buttons, checkboxes, and Likert-type questions. Open text is not a mandatory core indicator; it only appears when the participant selects ``other'' or the household voluntarily leaves a phone number for possible follow-up. The phone numbers are not included in the statistical analysis.

\textbf{Comparison objectives:} within-person pre/post deltas (Q2--Q1, for non-farmers only); post-test ratios of willingness to recommend, usefulness of AI, usefulness to students, authenticity of farmers, and changes in farmers' attitudes; distributions of burdens, compliance, and emissions classified by paths and final types; and behavior logs from completed simulations that were not submitted for the post-test---presented separately as path/abandonment evidence, not as questionnaire evidence.

\subsection{Simulation algorithm and scoring formulas}

The sandbox uses deterministic scoring formulas implemented in \texttt{index.html}. Its emissions accounting draws on greenhouse-gas inventory guidance, official regional electricity emission-factor tables, and the rural-building operational carbon-emission grading standard \cite{mee2011ghg,mee2020grid,cabr2025rural}. Let \(I\) be annual household income, \(S_t\) annual surplus after turn \(t\), \(A\) housing area in m\textsuperscript{2}, \(N\) household population, \(C_t\) annual heating cost, and \(C_0\) pre-transition coal heating cost. The displayed energy-burden rate is recalculated after each cost or income update:

\begin{equation}
B_t = 100 \times \frac{C_t}{\max(1,I_t)} .
\end{equation}

Here \(B_t\) is the heating-cost share of income. The denominator uses \(\max(1,I_t)\) only to avoid division by zero in the web app. A burden above 10\% with non-positive surplus triggers an early financial-strain ending; the same 10\% burden threshold is also used in the final success rule.

The initial coal baseline converts the user-entered coal heating cost into coal tons, input energy, and baseline winter CO\textsubscript{2}:

\begin{align}
Q_{\mathrm{coal}} &= \frac{C_0}{p_{\mathrm{coal}}}, \\
G_{\mathrm{coal}} &= Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times e_{\mathrm{coal}}, \\
E_0 &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{1000} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{coal}} .
\end{align}

In these equations, \(p_{\mathrm{coal}}=1000\) yuan/ton is the scattered-coal price calibrated from public/media accounts of rural coal heating costs, \(e_{\mathrm{coal}}=23.2\) GJ/ton is the coal input-energy constant used by the sandbox, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{coal}}=89.0\) tCO\textsubscript{2}/TJ is the coal CO\textsubscript{2} factor listed for general coal in the village-building carbon standard's fossil-fuel appendix \cite{cabr2025rural}. Dividing by 1000 converts GJ to TJ.

In the technology-route turn, all one-time retrofit costs scale linearly from a 100 m\textsuperscript{2} baseline:

\begin{equation}
K_r = k_r \times \frac{A}{100},
\end{equation}

where \(r\) is the chosen route. The constants are \(k_{\mathrm{gas}}=3000\), \(k_{\mathrm{ground}}=10000\), and \(k_{\mathrm{air}}=7000\) yuan per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}; these cost constants are calibrated from public clean-heating project descriptions and media/interview reports rather than treated as engineering price quotations. Route-specific annual heating cost is then estimated from the initial coal baseline:

\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{gas}} &= \operatorname{round}(Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times v_{\mathrm{gas}} \times p_{\mathrm{gas}}), \\
C_{\mathrm{ground}} &= \operatorname{round}\left(\frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{h_{\mathrm{ground}}} \times p_{\mathrm{elec}}\right), \\
C_{\mathrm{air}} &= \operatorname{round}\left(\frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{h_{\mathrm{air}}} \times p_{\mathrm{elec}}\right).
\end{align}

The constants are \(v_{\mathrm{gas}}=250\) m\textsuperscript{3}/ton coal, \(p_{\mathrm{gas}}=2.98\) yuan/m\textsuperscript{3}, \(p_{\mathrm{elec}}=0.5\) yuan/kWh, \(h_{\mathrm{ground}}=0.0144\) GJ/kWh, and \(h_{\mathrm{air}}=0.0106\) GJ/kWh. Gas/electricity prices and the gas-equivalent conversion are calibrated from public tariff information and media/interview reports; the heat-pump constants are simplified performance parameters used to translate the coal input-energy baseline into electricity demand. After route selection, the user's surplus is updated by subtracting the retrofit investment and the increase in annual heating cost relative to the original coal bill:

\begin{equation}
S_3 = S_2 - \left(K_r + C_r - C_0\right).
\end{equation}

Compliance is a 0--100 score updated by turn rules. Continuing coal in turn 1 sets compliance to 0; expressing willingness to transition sets it to 60. Continuing non-compliance in turn 2 keeps it at 0, while applying for rectification sets it to 50 and subtracts an 800 yuan application/preparation cost from surplus. These compliance and preparation-cost values are rule-based scores calibrated from policy descriptions and media/interview reports, not statutory penalty amounts. Completing a clean-heating retrofit sets compliance to 100:

\begin{equation}
L_t \in \{0,50,60,100\}.
\end{equation}

The enforcement branch uses a probability model only after at least two consecutive non-rectification choices and only when compliance is below 30. Housing area increases exposure:

\begin{align}
\mathrm{BP}(A) &= 40 + 15 \times \frac{A}{100}, \\
p_{\mathrm{escape}} &= 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BP}(A)}{100}, \\
p_{\mathrm{seized}}(m,A) &= 1 - p_{\mathrm{escape}}^{m}.
\end{align}

Here \(m\) is the consecutive non-rectification streak. The constants 40 and 15 are the base exposure weight and the additional area weight per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}, calibrated from media/interview descriptions of repeated village visits and larger-household visibility. If a random draw is below \(p_{\mathrm{seized}}\), the session ends in enforcement/seizure.

The CO\textsubscript{2} model recalculates emissions after route and energy-saving changes. Let \(f\) be the operating multiplier: \(f=1\) by default, \(f=0.6\) for the energy-saving option, and \(f=0.625\) after insulation repair. Current emissions are:

\begin{align}
E_{\mathrm{coal}} &= E_0 f, \\
E_{\mathrm{gas}} &= \frac{Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times v_{\mathrm{gas}} \times f \times e_{\mathrm{gas}}}{1000} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{gas}}, \\
E_{\mathrm{ground}} &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}} f}{h_{\mathrm{ground}}} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{grid}} \times 0.001, \\
E_{\mathrm{air}} &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}} f}{h_{\mathrm{air}}} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{grid}} \times 0.001.
\end{align}

The constants are \(e_{\mathrm{gas}}=0.036\) GJ/m\textsuperscript{3}, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{gas}}=55.54\) tCO\textsubscript{2}/TJ, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{grid}}=0.6361\) kgCO\textsubscript{2}/kWh. The natural-gas CO\textsubscript{2} factor is from the village-building carbon standard's fossil-fuel appendix, and the grid factor follows official regional electricity-emission-factor materials for China \cite{mee2020grid,cabr2025rural}. The heat-pump formulas multiply by 0.001 to convert kg to tons. The displayed emission score is a clipped 0--100 score based on per-capita annual heating emissions:

\begin{equation}
M_t = \min\left(100,\max\left(0,\operatorname{round}\left(100 - 20 \times \frac{E_t/N}{1.0}\right)\right)\right).
\end{equation}

The score denominator, 1.0 tCO\textsubscript{2} per capita, follows the cold-region low-carbon village limit for per-capita building carbon emissions in the village-building carbon standard; the score slope of 20 converts this standard-style threshold into a 0--100 in-game indicator rather than a formal certification grade \cite{cabr2025rural}. Thus higher per-capita heating emissions lower the score, while clean-heating routes or reduced operation can raise it.

Turn 4 contains three household-strategy algorithms. Income boosting increases both income and surplus by 20\% of current income:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I = \Delta S = \operatorname{round}(0.2 I_t).
\end{equation}

Energy saving sets annual heating cost to 60\% of the previous value and adds the saving to surplus:

\begin{align}
C_{t+1} &= \operatorname{round}(0.6 C_t), \\
S_{t+1} &= S_t + (C_t - C_{t+1}).
\end{align}

Insulation repair costs 1000 yuan per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}, then reduces annual heating demand to 62.5\% of the previous value. The cost and demand-reduction values are simplified calibrations from rural-building retrofit descriptions and media/interview reports:

\begin{align}
K_{\mathrm{ins}} &= 1000 \times \frac{A}{100}, \\
C_{t+1} &= \operatorname{round}(0.625 C_t), \\
S_{t+1} &= S_t - K_{\mathrm{ins}}.
\end{align}

Finally, turn 5 applies a subsidy phase-out shock using gas-equivalent demand inferred from the original coal bill:

\begin{align}
V_{\mathrm{gas}} &= Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times 250, \\
\Delta C_{\mathrm{subsidy}} &= \operatorname{round}(V_{\mathrm{gas}} \times 0.6), \\
C_5 &= C_4 + \Delta C_{\mathrm{subsidy}}.
\end{align}

The 0.6 yuan/m\textsuperscript{3} constant represents a subsidy-reduction shock calibrated from media/interview reports about operating-subsidy phase-out and gas-price pass-through. The terminal success condition requires all of the following:

\begin{equation}
M_5 \ge 80,\quad L_5 \ge 100,\quad B_5 \le 10,\quad S_5 \ge 0.
\end{equation}

If these conditions hold, the terminal outcome is successful transition. If surplus is negative, the outcome is financial strain. Otherwise, the simulator also records financial strain because the household fails at least one affordability or scoring condition after subsidy withdrawal.

\subsection{Recruitment, coding \& analysis}

\textbf{Recruitment and Sample:} There are a total of \textbf{53 original conversation records} in Supabase. The collection period is from \textbf{June 28th to July 5th, 2026}. The participants include students, rural farmers in North China (those who have been renovated and those who haven't), and other public users who are concerned about rural clean heating. The recruitment was carried out through course mobilization, public links, and online sharing. The distribution of original identities is as follows: 19 students, 15 renovated farmers, 8 unrenovated farmers, and 11 other users. This study only analyzes web conversations, questionnaire responses, and behavioral logs.

\textbf{Data cleaning and grouping:} Supabase records were exported and labeled by \texttt{export\_simulation\_sessions.py}. Test entries were defined as records where the \texttt{identity\_detail} matched the preset test labels and were excluded. Records with session duration less than 30 seconds or without \texttt{ended\_at} were not included in the formal analysis. Records with duration of at least 30 seconds and reaching the final stage, but lacking post-test, or non-farmers lacking Q1/Q2 were marked as \textbf{B\_valid incomplete}. Records that were ended and submitted with post-test were marked as \textbf{A\_fully complete}; for students/other users, A type also needs to have Q1 and Q2 at the same time. Finally, we obtained: \textbf{21 A records} for the main questionnaire analysis (students 7, other users 7, renovated farmers 4, unrenovated farmers 3), \textbf{7 B records} only for path/abandonment analysis (all farmers), and \textbf{25 C records} excluded from the formal analysis (23 records too short or incomplete sessions, 2 test entries).

\textbf{Statistical analysis:} A records represent the main questionnaire samples; B records are only used to describe the path selection, final outcome, and post-test dropout. For the paired Q1/Q2 in 14 non-farm A records, report the mean changes and improvement ratios of pre-test and post-test scores, and use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a small sample ordinal sensitivity check. The post-test classification questions are reported in terms of counts and percentages, including willingness to recommend, usefulness of AI, usefulness of student evaluation, authenticity of transformed farmers, attitude changes of untransformed farmers, and preference for paths. The behavioral path analysis uses the A+B valid pool: statistically analyze the technical choices in the sessions reaching the 3rd round, and unify the remaining labels as natural gas, ground source heat pump, and air source heat pump. Report the final outcomes for all valid completed simulation reports. The simulation indicators (energy burden, compliance, emission score, heating cost) are summarized by identity, path, and final type using median or mean. Due to the small sample size and as a self-selected sample, the comparisons between groups are exploratory; simple classification comparisons can use the Fisher's exact test, but this article does not claim causal effects. The optional ``Other'' text is only used as an anonymized context statement and is not a formal coded qualitative dataset; phone numbers are not included in the analysis.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the results mean}

The results show that the main value of this study does not lie in proving that a specific ``coal-to-X'' pathway is universally applicable, but in breaking down the broad question of whether clean heating is ``good'' into aspects such as household budget, technical path, compliance pressure, and final outcome. In the A+B effective pool, before users reach the path selection stage, they will move towards natural gas, air-source heat pumps, ground-source heat pumps, or enter the legal-related outcome. Under the same set of simulation rules, the final outcome will also be divided into successful transformation, financial strain, and law enforcement/forfeiture. This means that the simulation model has transformed the path selection issue raised in the introduction into recordable and comparable household-level data. Subsequently, the AI layer gives these data meaning for users: it can convert the farmer's income, savings, housing area, cost history, choices, and final status into a family profile, and explain why a certain path is more suitable or not suitable for a household.

The results also indicate that the interactive format has a practical educational effect. The overall comprehension scores of non-farmers increased before and after the test, and there were positive changes among both students and other stakeholders. In the post-test, most participants were willing to recommend the simulator, and 17 out of 21 people believed that the AI replay was helpful. Users did not merely complete a set of game procedures; after the experience, they could identify the specific variables behind the clean heating decisions, such as subsidy reduction, operating costs, initial investment during renovation, and family differences. The AI replay helped reconnect these variables to the user's own path, converting the original numbers into suggestions regarding affordability, compliance risks, and emission performance.

\subsection{Fit with the research question and hypotheses}

The question raised in the introduction is: Can the household-level simulation supported by AI help different users understand the trade-offs between environmental goals, affordability, and compliance, and produce evidence of path comparisons and personalized path recommendations? The results generally provide a positive answer. For non-farmers, the pre- and post-test data and questionnaire feedback show that their understanding of the cost structure of ``coal-to-X'' transformation and household differences has improved. For farmers, the simulator recorded real household inputs, path choices, final results, and post-test feedback, forming a data basis, enabling AI to describe the situation of this farmer and recommend a transformation direction that is more balanced between low-carbon goals and economic and livelihood constraints.

H1 Prediction: Farmers can utilize the simulation to identify the ``coal-to-X'' transformation path that better suits their family conditions. The current results provide formative evidence supporting H1: The farmers' records contain multiple path options, and different paths correspond to different final outcomes and indicators; the post-test of farmers also recorded the authenticity of costs, concerns about the transformation, and preferences for the path. However, H1 should not be interpreted as proving that a certain path is truly optimal for a real family, because this study did not track the actual expenditures after the transformation or the long-term heating behavior.

H2 Prediction: After a single experience, students and other non-farm users will have a better understanding of the dynamic changes in heating costs. The current results provide more direct support for H2: Among the 14 pairs of non-farm records, 11 showed an increase, 3 remained the same, and 0 decreased; the average score rose from 2.07 to 3.57. Combined with students' evaluations of usefulness and the ratings given by other stakeholders for ``understanding of family differences'', this indicates that the interactive simulation makes it easier for users to see: why the same policy leads to different outcomes in different families.

\subsection{Implications for village clean-heating decisions}

These results address the core tension mentioned in the introduction: Clean heating can reduce coal consumption and carbon dioxide emissions, but the success of the transition depends on the affordability at the household level. In the results, the median carbon dioxide emissions for successful transitions were lower and the emission scores were higher; however, after the technological transformation, a financial strain outcome could still occur. Therefore, village-level decision-making should not stop at whether to install ``coal-to-X'' equipment; it also needs to compare the energy burden, annual heating costs, and savings changes under different household types and paths.

Therefore, this simulator should be understood as a low-cost trial and recommendation environment, rather than a substitute for government or household decision-making. A village can first collect the input from each household and the simulation path choices, and then observe: under each path, which households are more likely to fall into financial strain, and which households can still maintain a lower burden after the subsidy reduction. At the individual level, AI retrospection can provide a more realistic way to interpret the ``profiles'' of farmers---for example, households with low savings and sensitive to previous investments; households with large housing areas and high operating costs; or households with greater non-compliance risks than short-term cost savings. Such household-level analysis helps to shift the path selection from a ``one-size-fits-all'' approach to recommendations that can both reduce emissions and protect affordability and daily winter heating needs.

\subsection{Limitations and boundaries}

First, the sample size is small, and the participants were recruited through course mobilization, public links, and online sharing, and chose to participate voluntarily. Therefore, it cannot represent the entire rural area of North China. The main questionnaire A recorded only 21 cases, and the valid pool of farmers A+B was 14 cases. Thus, these findings should be read more as evidence of feasibility and formative effects rather than conclusive or regionally representative conclusions.

Second, the pre-test and post-test understanding is only applicable to students and other non-farmers; farmers did not complete the same pre-test. As a result, the evidence for H2 is more direct than that for H1. Currently, H1 mainly relies on behavioral logs, path choices, final outcomes, and post-test feedback. A stronger future test for H1 requires a larger farmer sample and pre-test measurements of farmers' path preferences.

Thirdly, the applicable and calibration range of this simulator is mainly limited to the rural clean heating scenarios in North China. The geographical and policy scope is relatively small, and it cannot be directly extrapolated to other climate zones, energy structures, or subsidy systems for heating transition issues. The simulator cannot completely replace the real household account books. It only provides reference opinions based on household conditions and system algorithms.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of conclusions}

This study argues that the transition to clean heating in rural areas of North China should not be framed as a single question of ``whether it is worth doing clean heating or not'', but rather as a comparative issue: Under specific household conditions, which ``coal-to-X'' path can be affordable, sustainable, compliant, and also achieve emission reduction? Through five rounds of interactive simulators, this study placed income, housing area, initial heating costs, technical paths, subsidy reduction, compliance pressure, and final indicators in the same available scenario. The results showed that non-farm households' understanding improved after experiencing it; farmers' records produced different path choices and final outcomes. Therefore, this simulator is a tool for collecting household-level data, demonstrating path differences, and providing solution selection suggestions for those in need.
\subsection{Implications}

Theoretically, this study adds a form of evidence that is still uncommon in clean heating research: interactive evidence at the household level. Existing studies have demonstrated environmental benefits and identified risks such as subsidy withdrawal, operating costs, and coal rebound \cite{liu2025pahs,yuan2025carbon,zhang2024uneven,zhao2024sustainable,liu2020costs,zhai2023review,hu2021decoalizing}. This project shows how these variables can be integrated into village-level decision-making sandboxes that are accessible to ordinary users, and its outputs can be recorded and summarized. It takes policy simulation one step forward from expert models and regional statistics, towards tools that are public-oriented, experiential, and capable of generating data.

In practice, this simulator can support early calculations for path selection at the village and town levels. Villages do not have to rely solely on a single technological path or average household data; they can collect data from each household and compare energy burdens, annual heating costs, emission scores, and financial risk under different paths. For farmers, the simulator can make subsidy reduction, upfront renovation investment, and long-term operation costs visible before formal renovation decisions are made. For students and the broader public, it converts abstract energy policies into specific household budgets and enhances public understanding.

\subsection{Future directions}

Future work should extend the sandbox from North China clean heating to a global decision tool for both heating and cooling. In many overseas settings---especially among farm households in Europe and North America---energy choices are highly individual. Unlike rural China, where village- or township-level campaigns often coordinate a common route, those households mainly decide for themselves and face fewer layered subsidy-phase-out rules. Cross-country technology information gaps remain large: a household abroad may not know that a route already used in China fits its climate, resources, and budget. The next version of the tool should therefore take regional natural conditions, resource endowments, and household data as inputs, run algorithm-based route scoring together with AI analysis of local and family conditions, and return fitness scores and plain-language recommendations across the full set of candidate heating and cooling pathways. On that basis, the same framework can help users compare ``which path fits this household and this place'' rather than assuming one universal technology.
