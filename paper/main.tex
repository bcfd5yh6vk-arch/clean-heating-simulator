\section{Introduction}

I grew up in Beijing since childhood and witnessed firsthand how the air quality of this city changed from frequent smog in winter to a significantly cleaner sky. This improvement made me believe that the clean heating policy was successful and necessary. However, recent public discussions, as well as my field research in a rural city in Hebei Province, have led me to face a more challenging issue: Part of the improvement in urban environment might come at the cost of rural families in North China having to bear higher winter heating costs and facing new transformation risks.

Winter heating is a basic livelihood need in rural areas of North China. Since the mid-2010s, when the country promoted clean heating, tens of millions of farmers have shifted from burning coal to using gas, electricity, or heat pumps as part of the ``coal-to-X'' transformation \cite{li2025scatteredcoal,ndrc2017plan}. Regional air quality has improved, and winter coal consumption has significantly decreased. However, the environmental benefits do not automatically translate into sustainable energy use for every household. During a rural survey in Hebei Province, I found that even within the same village, each household varies in terms of income, housing area, savings, heating habits, equipment conditions, and risk tolerance. What is affordable for one household may become a heavy burden for another.

The specificity of the policy benefits lies in the fact that China's clean heating initiatives are usually organized and implemented at the village and town levels rather than being decided on a household-by-household basis. Upper-level decision-makers often lack detailed data on the optimal heating path for each household, so projects that are technically ``completed'' may still deviate from the daily needs of the people. During the initial stage of renovation, the investment and the regular winter bills are mainly borne by the households; as the equipment subsidies and operation subsidies gradually decrease in batches, many households find themselves in a familiar dilemma: they can afford to install the equipment, but they cannot afford to use it. The proportion of heating expenses in the annual income has increased, the indoor temperature has dropped, and some households have returned to burning coal or maintaining dual fuel usage.

The disparity between environmental benefits and household affordability has drawn attention from both domestic and international media; media reports have also made more people aware of the difficulties of rural clean heating. However, at the same time, many observers still do not understand the underlying mechanism, and some online discussions are easily influenced by one-sided or false information. Existing research has documented this tension from multiple perspectives: Environmental and health studies show that clean heating can reduce PM\textsubscript{2.5} exposure, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon pollution and carbon emissions, but the benefits and costs are not evenly distributed among households \cite{liu2025pahs,yuan2025carbon,zhang2024uneven}; Energy economy and energy poverty studies have found that if gas expenses exceed about 5\% of disposable income, it will significantly change behavior, and the withdrawal of subsidies will increase winter costs and expand the proportion of households facing affordability pressure \cite{zhao2024sustainable,liu2020costs,zhai2023review}; Field evidence has also reported county-level subsidy differences, electricity/gas price pass-through, equipment replacement cycles and coal rebound \cite{hu2021decoalizing}. Regional policy and household choice research links household actions with results at the village and city levels, but tools that allow users to input household income, housing area and heating costs, interactively compare natural gas, heat pumps and insulation paths, and experience subsidy withdrawal and compliance pressure in a single session are still rare.

This study was specifically designed to address this gap, and it developed, deployed, and evaluated a \textbf{village-level clean heating transformation simulation sandbox} (\url{https://www.clean-heating-simulator.com}). Participants entered the scenario as farmers, entered their family income, savings, housing area, and pre-transition heating costs, and then made choices in five rounds---whether to transform, which technology to adopt, how to increase income or save energy, how to deal with the withdrawal of subsidies---while viewing real-time indicators such as heating costs, energy burden rate, legal compliance, and emission scores. The AI in this case is not a decorative chatbot that appears at the end of the game; it reads user input, step-by-step choices, and final outcomes, and translates these data trajectories into farmer profiles, including income pressure, housing size, cost affordability, compliance risks, and vulnerability to subsidy reduction; based on this, it explains which heating transformation path is more appropriate and why, enabling discussions on low-carbon emission reduction, economic affordability, and livelihood security. The simulation engine itself is deterministic and calibrated based on literature and public statistics; the AI adds personalized explanations and suggestions, without changing the scoring rules.

The research question is: \textit{Can the household-level simulation supported by AI help users of different identities---students, farmers who have undergone transformation and those who have not, and other stakeholders---understand the trade-offs between environmental goals, affordability of heating, and compliance, and provide household-level evidence and personalized path recommendations?} We assume: (H1) Farmer users can identify the coal-to-X transformation path that better suits their own household conditions; (H2) Students and other non-farmer users will have an improved understanding of the dynamic aspects of heating costs after one experience. From June 28th to July 5th, 2026, we recruited 21 complete case studies (including rural users in the administrative regions of Hebei Province and Beijing) through course mobilization and public links, using pre- and post-test questionnaires, and storing behavioral logs in Supabase.

The entire paper is structured as follows: The literature review summarizes the evidence in the areas of environmental benefits, affordability pressure, technological choices, and policy simulations; The methods section explains the deployed products, designs, informed consents, and analysis plans; The results section reports the data from questionnaires, logs, and simulation outcomes; The discussion section interprets how these results address the research questions and H1/H2, and points out the limitations; The conclusion section summarizes the contributions, implications, limitations, and future directions.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Environmental benefits of clean heating}

Previous studies have first established the environmental significance of ``coal-to-X'' heating. The national clean heating policy aims to replace rural coal usage and defines it as a key path to reduce winter air pollution in the north and increase the coverage of clean heating \cite{ndrc2017plan,li2025scatteredcoal}. Empirical research on rural clean heating also shows that replacing coal can reduce PM\textsubscript{2.5}, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH), and carbon emission pressure \cite{liu2025pahs,yuan2025carbon}; however, the distribution of health benefits and economic costs varies among different households \cite{zhang2024uneven}. Therefore, clean heating is not only a technical replacement at the household level, but also a component of air pollution control, carbon reduction, and rural energy transition.

\subsection{Affordability, energy poverty, and coal rebound}

The second type of research indicates that environmental benefits are often accompanied by cost pressures at the household level. A review of the comprehensive renovation of clean heating in northern rural areas identified high operating costs, weak infrastructure, and ``coal rebound'' as the main obstacles \cite{zhai2023review}. The research by Liu and Mauzerall on the cost of clean heating showed that in the absence of subsidies, the gas and electricity paths could be much more expensive than burning coal \cite{liu2020costs}. Wang et al. further discovered that the withdrawal of subsidies would increase winter heating expenses and expand the proportion of households facing affordability pressure \cite{zhao2024sustainable}. Hu's research on the ``2+26'' region also recorded coal rebound and reliance on subsidies \cite{hu2021decoalizing}. In summary, these studies indicate that transformation cannot be considered sustainable merely because equipment has been installed; households must also be able to afford daily winter use.

\subsection{Technology routes and village-level choice}

The third type of research focuses on the differences among various ``coal-to-X'' transformation paths. Gas, electric heating, air-source heat pumps, ground-source heat pumps, and insulation renovations vary in terms of initial investment, operating costs, comfort level, reliance on infrastructure, and maintenance. Existing cost and policy studies indicate that clean heating renovations should be matched with local resources, household income, building conditions, and operating costs \cite{liu2020costs,zhao2024sustainable,zhai2023review,qin2026heating}. Since many rural heating transitions are organized and implemented at the village and town levels, if a single path does not suit the majority of households, a gap will arise between ``policy completion'' and ``family affordability''.

\subsection{Policy simulation, AI methods, and this study's gap}

The fourth type of literature provides a basis for the methods adopted in this study. Based on the agent-based and multi-agent simulation methods, individual behaviors can be linked to the results of group or regional policies \cite{wang2023abm,ma2024agi}. Both policy studies and research on family choices have shown how individual heating decisions interact with regional clean heating outcomes \cite{hu2021decoalizing,zhang2024uneven}. Existing research can demonstrate environmental benefits, identify affordable risks, and compare technical paths. However, there are still few tools that are accessible to the public, allowing farmers and other users to input household income, housing area, and heating costs, and compare natural gas, heat pumps, insulation, subsidy reduction, and compliance pressure in the same interface. This study fills this gap by converting regional policy issues into village-level decision-making sandboxes at the household level, in a round-robin format, with recorded data.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Artifact}

We deployed a \textbf{village-level clean-heating transition simulation sandbox} for rural North China coal-to-X contexts (\url{https://www.clean-heating-simulator.com}; single-page \texttt{index.html} on Vercel). Participants need no account. After selecting an identity on the welcome screen (student, converted farmer, unconverted farmer, or other stakeholder) and entering basic background, they create a North China farm household profile: \textbf{whole-household} annual income, annual surplus, floor area, resident population, and pre-transition winter heating cost. Role (farm household), region (North China), and initial heating mode (scattered coal) are fixed.

The simulation runs five turns: (1) status quo choice (continue coal or prepare transition); (2) compliance pressure (probabilistic home visit if coal continues, scaled by floor area and consecutive non-compliance); (3) technology route (natural gas, ground-source heat pump, or air-source heat pump; retrofit and operating costs linearly scaled from a 100 m\textsuperscript{2} baseline); (4) income boost or energy saving (one of three options); (5) subsidy phase-out (equivalent gas volume derived from initial coal spending, then a fixed per-m\textsuperscript{3} cost increase, followed by terminal adjudication). The interface displays six live indicators---annual heating cost, income, surplus, legal compliance (0--100), emission score (0--100), and energy burden rate (\%)---plus a turn-by-turn narrative log. Terminal outcomes are successful transition, financial strain, or enforcement-related seizure.

After the terminal state, participants may request \textbf{AI debrief}: the browser POSTs initial inputs, stepwise logs, and final state to \texttt{/api/chat}; the server calls the \textbf{DeepSeek Chat Completions API} (\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}) with one of three \textbf{human-authored structured system prompts} (converted-farmer comparison / unconverted-farmer retrofit advice / default analysis). The model must use only logged numbers and return three Markdown sections. If the API key is missing or the call fails, the UI shows an error---\textbf{no curated mock script is served as fallback}. The simulation engine itself is deterministic; emission factors are anchored in official electricity-factor data and a village-building carbon standard, while economic, compliance, and policy-shock constants are calibrated from literature, public statistics, and media/interview reports (\texttt{algori\_spec.md}, \texttt{research/data/calibration\_defaults.json}).

Each session is stored anonymously (UUID) in Supabase \texttt{simulation\_sessions}, including baseline inputs, turn-choice IDs/labels, \texttt{event\_log}, terminal type/summary, final metrics, AI analysis text (if generated), and post-survey fields.

\subsection{Design}

We used a \textbf{single-session exposure design} combining pre/post questionnaires and behavioral logging. The intervention was completing one full sandbox run (optional AI debrief and post-survey).

\textbf{Independent variables (IVs):} (a) exposure to the live sandbox and self-selected pathway (technology, compliance branch, income/energy choices---not experimenter-assigned); (b) participant identity (student, converted farmer, unconverted farmer, other) as a between-subjects factor.

\textbf{Dependent variables (DVs):} (1) \textbf{Survey cognition/attitudes}---for students and other non-farmers: pre Q1 and post Q2 (5-point Likert understanding of coal-to-gas and heating costs), Q4 (strategy change if replayed), S1 (student-rated simulator helpfulness), O2 (other-audience understanding of household variation), recommendation intent, and AI helpfulness; for converted farmers: current heating method, post-transition cost band, cost-saving behavior, cost realism (F4), best/worst-matching stages (F6--F7), and subsidy coping; for unconverted farmers: current heating method, concerns (N2), attitude shift (N3), and preferred route (N4). (2) \textbf{Behavioral logs}---five-turn choices, whether the user reached technology-route selection, gas/ground-source heat-pump/air-source heat-pump selection, and terminal type. (3) \textbf{Simulated outcomes}---terminal energy burden, heating cost, surplus, compliance, emission score, and CO\textsubscript{2} tons.

\textbf{Pre/post structure:} Non-farmers completed a \textbf{one-item pre-survey} (Q1) before simulation and a \textbf{\textasciitilde{}2-minute post-survey} after AI debrief. \textbf{Converted and unconverted farmers skipped the pre-survey} and only completed the post-survey. The latest post-survey mainly uses radio buttons, checkboxes, and Likert items. Open text is not a required core measure; it appears only when a participant selects an ``other'' option or, for farmers, voluntarily agrees to leave a phone number for possible follow-up. Phone numbers are not included in statistical analysis.

\textbf{Comparison targets:} within-person pre/post deltas (Q2--Q1, non-farmers only); post-survey rates for recommendation intent, AI helpfulness, student helpfulness, farmer realism, and farmer attitude shift; distributions of burden/compliance/emission by route and terminal type; and behavior logs from completed simulations that lacked post-survey, reported separately as route/dropout evidence rather than as survey evidence.

\subsection{Simulation algorithm and scoring formulas}

The sandbox uses deterministic scoring formulas implemented in \texttt{index.html}. Its emissions accounting draws on greenhouse-gas inventory guidance, official regional electricity emission-factor tables, and the rural-building operational carbon-emission grading standard \cite{mee2011ghg,mee2020grid,cabr2025rural}. Let \(I\) be annual household income, \(S_t\) annual surplus after turn \(t\), \(A\) housing area in m\textsuperscript{2}, \(N\) household population, \(C_t\) annual heating cost, and \(C_0\) pre-transition coal heating cost. The displayed energy-burden rate is recalculated after each cost or income update:

\begin{equation}
B_t = 100 \times \frac{C_t}{\max(1,I_t)} .
\end{equation}

Here \(B_t\) is the heating-cost share of income. The denominator uses \(\max(1,I_t)\) only to avoid division by zero in the web app. A burden above 10\% with non-positive surplus triggers an early financial-strain ending; the same 10\% burden threshold is also used in the final success rule.

The initial coal baseline converts the user-entered coal heating cost into coal tons, input energy, and baseline winter CO\textsubscript{2}:

\begin{align}
Q_{\mathrm{coal}} &= \frac{C_0}{p_{\mathrm{coal}}}, \\
G_{\mathrm{coal}} &= Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times e_{\mathrm{coal}}, \\
E_0 &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{1000} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{coal}} .
\end{align}

In these equations, \(p_{\mathrm{coal}}=1000\) yuan/ton is the scattered-coal price calibrated from public/media accounts of rural coal heating costs, \(e_{\mathrm{coal}}=23.2\) GJ/ton is the coal input-energy constant used by the sandbox, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{coal}}=89.0\) tCO\textsubscript{2}/TJ is the coal CO\textsubscript{2} factor listed for general coal in the village-building carbon standard's fossil-fuel appendix \cite{cabr2025rural}. Dividing by 1000 converts GJ to TJ.

In the technology-route turn, all one-time retrofit costs scale linearly from a 100 m\textsuperscript{2} baseline:

\begin{equation}
K_r = k_r \times \frac{A}{100},
\end{equation}

where \(r\) is the chosen route. The constants are \(k_{\mathrm{gas}}=3000\), \(k_{\mathrm{ground}}=10000\), and \(k_{\mathrm{air}}=7000\) yuan per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}; these cost constants are calibrated from public clean-heating project descriptions and media/interview reports rather than treated as engineering price quotations. Route-specific annual heating cost is then estimated from the initial coal baseline:

\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{gas}} &= \operatorname{round}(Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times v_{\mathrm{gas}} \times p_{\mathrm{gas}}), \\
C_{\mathrm{ground}} &= \operatorname{round}\left(\frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{h_{\mathrm{ground}}} \times p_{\mathrm{elec}}\right), \\
C_{\mathrm{air}} &= \operatorname{round}\left(\frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}}}{h_{\mathrm{air}}} \times p_{\mathrm{elec}}\right).
\end{align}

The constants are \(v_{\mathrm{gas}}=250\) m\textsuperscript{3}/ton coal, \(p_{\mathrm{gas}}=2.98\) yuan/m\textsuperscript{3}, \(p_{\mathrm{elec}}=0.5\) yuan/kWh, \(h_{\mathrm{ground}}=0.0144\) GJ/kWh, and \(h_{\mathrm{air}}=0.0106\) GJ/kWh. Gas/electricity prices and the gas-equivalent conversion are calibrated from public tariff information and media/interview reports; the heat-pump constants are simplified performance parameters used to translate the coal input-energy baseline into electricity demand. After route selection, the user's surplus is updated by subtracting the retrofit investment and the increase in annual heating cost relative to the original coal bill:

\begin{equation}
S_3 = S_2 - \left(K_r + C_r - C_0\right).
\end{equation}

Compliance is a 0--100 score updated by turn rules. Continuing coal in turn 1 sets compliance to 0; expressing willingness to transition sets it to 60. Continuing non-compliance in turn 2 keeps it at 0, while applying for rectification sets it to 50 and subtracts an 800 yuan application/preparation cost from surplus. These compliance and preparation-cost values are rule-based scores calibrated from policy descriptions and media/interview reports, not statutory penalty amounts. Completing a clean-heating retrofit sets compliance to 100:

\begin{equation}
L_t \in \{0,50,60,100\}.
\end{equation}

The enforcement branch uses a probability model only after at least two consecutive non-rectification choices and only when compliance is below 30. Housing area increases exposure:

\begin{align}
\mathrm{BP}(A) &= 40 + 15 \times \frac{A}{100}, \\
p_{\mathrm{escape}} &= 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BP}(A)}{100}, \\
p_{\mathrm{seized}}(m,A) &= 1 - p_{\mathrm{escape}}^{m}.
\end{align}

Here \(m\) is the consecutive non-rectification streak. The constants 40 and 15 are the base exposure weight and the additional area weight per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}, calibrated from media/interview descriptions of repeated village visits and larger-household visibility. If a random draw is below \(p_{\mathrm{seized}}\), the session ends in enforcement/seizure.

The CO\textsubscript{2} model recalculates emissions after route and energy-saving changes. Let \(f\) be the operating multiplier: \(f=1\) by default, \(f=0.6\) for the energy-saving option, and \(f=0.625\) after insulation repair. Current emissions are:

\begin{align}
E_{\mathrm{coal}} &= E_0 f, \\
E_{\mathrm{gas}} &= \frac{Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times v_{\mathrm{gas}} \times f \times e_{\mathrm{gas}}}{1000} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{gas}}, \\
E_{\mathrm{ground}} &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}} f}{h_{\mathrm{ground}}} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{grid}} \times 0.001, \\
E_{\mathrm{air}} &= \frac{G_{\mathrm{coal}} f}{h_{\mathrm{air}}} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{grid}} \times 0.001.
\end{align}

The constants are \(e_{\mathrm{gas}}=0.036\) GJ/m\textsuperscript{3}, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{gas}}=55.54\) tCO\textsubscript{2}/TJ, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{grid}}=0.6361\) kgCO\textsubscript{2}/kWh. The natural-gas CO\textsubscript{2} factor is from the village-building carbon standard's fossil-fuel appendix, and the grid factor follows official regional electricity-emission-factor materials for China \cite{mee2020grid,cabr2025rural}. The heat-pump formulas multiply by 0.001 to convert kg to tons. The displayed emission score is a clipped 0--100 score based on per-capita annual heating emissions:

\begin{equation}
M_t = \min\left(100,\max\left(0,\operatorname{round}\left(100 - 20 \times \frac{E_t/N}{1.0}\right)\right)\right).
\end{equation}

The score denominator, 1.0 tCO\textsubscript{2} per capita, follows the cold-region low-carbon village limit for per-capita building carbon emissions in the village-building carbon standard; the score slope of 20 converts this standard-style threshold into a 0--100 in-game indicator rather than a formal certification grade \cite{cabr2025rural}. Thus higher per-capita heating emissions lower the score, while clean-heating routes or reduced operation can raise it.

Turn 4 contains three household-strategy algorithms. Income boosting increases both income and surplus by 20\% of current income:

\begin{equation}
\Delta I = \Delta S = \operatorname{round}(0.2 I_t).
\end{equation}

Energy saving sets annual heating cost to 60\% of the previous value and adds the saving to surplus:

\begin{align}
C_{t+1} &= \operatorname{round}(0.6 C_t), \\
S_{t+1} &= S_t + (C_t - C_{t+1}).
\end{align}

Insulation repair costs 1000 yuan per 100 m\textsuperscript{2}, then reduces annual heating demand to 62.5\% of the previous value. The cost and demand-reduction values are simplified calibrations from rural-building retrofit descriptions and media/interview reports:

\begin{align}
K_{\mathrm{ins}} &= 1000 \times \frac{A}{100}, \\
C_{t+1} &= \operatorname{round}(0.625 C_t), \\
S_{t+1} &= S_t - K_{\mathrm{ins}}.
\end{align}

Finally, turn 5 applies a subsidy phase-out shock using gas-equivalent demand inferred from the original coal bill:

\begin{align}
V_{\mathrm{gas}} &= Q_{\mathrm{coal}} \times 250, \\
\Delta C_{\mathrm{subsidy}} &= \operatorname{round}(V_{\mathrm{gas}} \times 0.6), \\
C_5 &= C_4 + \Delta C_{\mathrm{subsidy}}.
\end{align}

The 0.6 yuan/m\textsuperscript{3} constant represents a subsidy-reduction shock calibrated from media/interview reports about operating-subsidy phase-out and gas-price pass-through. The terminal success condition requires all of the following:

\begin{equation}
M_5 \ge 80,\quad L_5 \ge 100,\quad B_5 \le 10,\quad S_5 \ge 0.
\end{equation}

If these conditions hold, the terminal outcome is successful transition. If surplus is negative, the outcome is financial strain. Otherwise, the simulator also records financial strain because the household fails at least one affordability or scoring condition after subsidy withdrawal.

\subsection{Consent}

Participation began with three first-screen layers: \textbf{welcome} (identity/background), \textbf{context briefing}, and \textbf{informed consent} (\texttt{consentOverlay}). The consent screen stated that data are \textbf{anonymous} (no name; phone not collected by default), participation is \textbf{voluntary}, users may \textbf{exit anytime}, and data are used \textbf{only to improve the simulator and for academic research} (not advertising). The main app unlocked only after clicking ``Agree.'' The tool is a \textbf{policy/livelihood decision simulation for public education}, not a clinical, diagnostic, or therapeutic intervention.

\subsection{Recruitment, coding \& analysis}

\textbf{Recruitment and sample:} Supabase contained \textbf{53 raw session records} collected from \textbf{June 28 to July 5, 2026}. Participants included students, rural North China farmers (converted and unconverted), and other public users interested in rural clean heating, recruited through course outreach, a public URL, and online sharing. The raw identity distribution was 19 students, 15 converted farmers, 8 unconverted farmers, and 11 other users. The study analyzes only web sessions, survey responses, and behavioral logs.

\textbf{Data cleaning and grouping:} Supabase records were exported and annotated with \texttt{export\_simulation\_sessions.py}. Test entries were defined as records whose \texttt{identity\_detail} matched predefined test labels and were excluded. Records with session duration under 30 seconds or no \texttt{ended\_at} were also excluded from formal analysis. Records lasting at least 30 seconds and reaching an ending, but missing post-survey or missing Q1/Q2 for non-farmers, were labeled \textbf{B\_valid incomplete}. Records that ended and submitted post-survey were labeled \textbf{A\_fully complete}; for student/other users, A status also required both Q1 and Q2. This produced \textbf{21 A records} for the main survey analysis (7 students, 7 other users, 4 converted farmers, 3 unconverted farmers), \textbf{7 B records} for route/dropout analysis only (all farmers), and \textbf{25 C records} excluded from formal analysis (23 short or unfinished sessions and 2 test entries).

\textbf{Statistical analysis:} A records are the main questionnaire sample; B records are used only to describe route choice, terminal outcome, and post-survey dropout. For the 14 non-farmer A records with paired Q1/Q2, we report the mean pre/post change, the proportion that improved, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a small-sample ordinal sensitivity check. Post-survey categorical items are reported as counts and percentages, including recommendation intent, AI helpfulness, student-rated helpfulness, converted-farmer realism, unconverted-farmer attitude shift, and preferred path. Behavioral route analysis uses the A+B valid pool: technology choices are counted among sessions that reached turn 3, with legacy labels normalized into natural gas, ground-source heat pump, and air-source heat pump. Terminal outcomes are reported for all valid completed simulations. Simulated metrics (energy burden, compliance, emission score, and heating cost) are summarized by identity, route, and terminal type using medians or means. Because the sample is small and self-selected, group comparisons are exploratory; Fisher exact tests may be used for simple categorical contrasts, but the paper does not claim causal effects. Optional ``other'' text is used only as anonymized illustrative context, not as a formal coded qualitative dataset; phone numbers are excluded from analysis.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the results mean}

The results show that the main value of this study is not to prove that one coal-to-X route is universally best, but to break the broad question of whether clean heating is ``good'' into household budgets, technology routes, compliance pressure, and terminal outcomes. In the A+B valid pool, users moved toward natural gas, air-source heat pumps, ground-source heat pumps, or enforcement outcomes before reaching route selection. Under the same simulation rules, terminal outcomes also diverged into successful transition, financial strain, and enforcement/seizure. This means the sandbox turns the pathway-choice problem raised in the Introduction into recordable and comparable household-level data. The AI layer then gives those data a user-facing meaning: it can read a farmer's income, surplus, housing area, cost history, choices, and final state as a household profile, and explain why a specific route is more or less suitable for that household.

The results also show that the interactive format has a practical educational effect. Non-farmer pre/post understanding scores increased overall, with positive movement among both students and other stakeholders. In the post-survey, most participants were willing to recommend the simulator, and 17/21 rated the AI debrief as helpful. Users did not only finish a game flow; after the session, they could identify concrete variables behind clean-heating decisions, such as subsidy phase-out, operating cost, upfront retrofit cost, and household variation. The AI debrief helped connect these variables back to the user's own pathway, turning raw numbers into advice about affordability, compliance risk, and emission performance.

\subsection{Fit with the research question and hypotheses}

The Introduction asked whether AI-supported household simulation can help different users understand trade-offs among environmental goals, affordability, and compliance, while producing evidence for pathway comparison and personalized route advice. The results give an overall affirmative answer. For non-farmers, pre/post data and survey feedback show improved understanding of coal-to-X cost structure and household variation. For farmers, the simulator recorded real household inputs, route choices, terminal outcomes, and post-survey feedback, creating a data basis from which AI can describe the farmer's situation and recommend a transition direction that better balances low-carbon goals with economic and livelihood constraints.

H1 predicted that farmer users could use the simulation to identify coal-to-X paths better matched to their own household conditions. The current results support H1 as formative evidence: farmer records include multiple route choices, and different routes were associated with different terminal outcomes and metrics; farmer post-surveys also recorded cost realism, retrofit concerns, and preferred paths. However, H1 should not be read as proving that a specific route is truly optimal for a real household, because this study did not track actual post-retrofit spending or heating behavior over time.

H2 predicted that students and other non-farmer users would improve their understanding of heating-cost dynamics after one session. The current results support H2 more directly: among 14 paired non-farmer records, 11 increased, 3 stayed the same, and none decreased; the mean score rose from 2.07 to 3.57. Together with student-rated helpfulness and other stakeholders' ratings of household-variation understanding, this suggests that interactive simulation made it easier for users to see why the same policy can produce different household outcomes.

\subsection{Implications for village clean-heating decisions}

These results speak to the central tension in the Introduction: clean heating can reduce coal burning and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions, but transition stability depends on household-level affordability. In the results, successful-transition outcomes had lower median CO\textsubscript{2} and higher emission scores, while financial-strain outcomes could still occur after technical conversion. Village decisions therefore cannot stop at whether coal-to-X equipment is installed; they also need to compare energy burden, annual heating cost, and surplus changes across household types and routes.

The simulator should therefore be understood as a low-cost trial and advisory environment, not as a replacement for government or household decision-making. A village could first collect household inputs and simulated route choices, then observe which households are more likely to face financial strain under each route and which can maintain lower burden after subsidy phase-out. At the individual level, the AI debrief can explain the farmer's ``image'' in practical terms---for example, a low-surplus household sensitive to upfront investment, a large-area home exposed to high operating cost, or a household whose non-compliance risk outweighs short-term savings. Such household-level analysis can help pathway choice move from one-size-fits-all implementation toward recommendations that reduce emissions while protecting affordability and daily winter heating needs.

\subsection{Limitations and boundaries}

First, the sample is small and self-selected through course outreach, a public link, and online sharing, so it cannot represent rural North China as a whole. The main A-record survey sample has only 21 cases, and the farmer A+B valid pool has 14 cases. The findings are therefore best read as evidence of feasibility and formative effect, not as strong causal or regionally representative conclusions.

Second, pre/post understanding was measured only for students and other non-farmers; farmer users did not complete the same pre-survey. As a result, H2 has more direct evidence than H1. H1 currently relies on behavioral logs, route choices, terminal outcomes, and post-survey feedback. A stronger future test of H1 would require a larger farmer sample and pre/post measurement of farmers' route preferences.

Third, the simulator uses a deterministic algorithm, literature constants, and simplified thresholds. It cannot fully replace real household accounts, equipment efficiency, building insulation, local gas/electricity prices, or county subsidy rules. The AI debrief is also an explanation generated from session logs, not an independent prediction model. The results should therefore be understood as an initial evaluation of an interactive policy-education and data-collection tool, not as a final estimate of real coal-to-X project benefits.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of conclusions}

This study concludes that rural North China's clean-heating transition should not be framed as a single question of whether clean heating is worthwhile. It should be treated as a comparative question: which coal-to-X pathway is affordable, maintainable, compliant, and emission-reducing under specific household conditions? Through a five-turn interactive simulator, this study placed income, floor area, initial heating cost, technology route, subsidy phase-out, compliance pressure, and terminal metrics into one usable scenario. The results show improved understanding among non-farmer users after the session, and farmer records produced different route choices and terminal outcomes. The simulator is therefore not only a policy-display website; it can also serve as a research tool for collecting household-level data, showing pathway differences, and supporting village comparison.

This conclusion is not a simple repetition of the numerical results. It synthesizes them into a more specific claim: interactive simulation can translate macro-level policy debate into household-level trade-offs among cost, emissions, and compliance. It cannot replace real policy evaluation, but it lets users preview the consequences of different choices in a low-risk setting, moving the question from ``Is coal-to-X good?'' to ``Which combination fits this household and this village?''

\subsection{Implications}

Theoretically, this study adds a form of household-level interactive evidence that is still uncommon in clean-heating research. Existing studies have shown environmental benefits and identified risks from subsidy withdrawal, operating cost, and coal rebound. This project shows how those variables can be integrated into a village decision sandbox that ordinary users can operate, and whose outputs can be recorded and aggregated. It moves policy simulation one step from expert models and regional statistics toward public-facing, experiential, and data-generating tools.

Practically, the simulator can support early-stage trial calculation for village- or township-level pathway choice. Villages do not need to rely only on one technology route or average household data; they can collect household inputs and compare energy burden, annual heating cost, emission score, and financial-strain risk across routes. For farmers, the simulator can make subsidy phase-out, upfront retrofit cost, and long-term operating cost visible before formal retrofit decisions. For students and the wider public, it turns abstract energy policy into a concrete household budget and improves public understanding.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has three main limitations. First, the sample is small and self-selected. The A-record main survey sample has 21 cases, and the farmer A+B valid pool has 14 cases, so the findings cannot represent all rural North China and should not be treated as strong causal evidence. Second, farmer users did not complete the same pre-survey as non-farmer users. As a result, H1 relies mainly on behavioral logs, route choices, and post-survey feedback, while H2 has more direct pre/post evidence. Third, the simulator uses literature constants and simplified rules, so it cannot fully capture local gas prices, electricity prices, subsidy rules, building insulation, equipment efficiency, or household labor differences.

In addition, although most users rated the AI debrief as helpful, it remains an explanatory text generated from session logs, not an independent policy prediction model. Its role is closer to helping users interpret their own simulated pathway than to replacing expert assessment or real engineering calculation.

\subsection{Future directions}

Future work should extend the sandbox from North China clean heating to a global decision tool for both heating and cooling. In many overseas settings---especially among farm households in Europe and North America---energy choices are highly individual. Unlike rural China, where village- or township-level campaigns often coordinate a common route, those households mainly decide for themselves and face fewer layered subsidy-phase-out rules. Cross-country technology information gaps remain large: a household abroad may not know that a route already used in China fits its climate, resources, and budget. The next version of the tool should therefore take regional natural conditions, resource endowments, and household data as inputs, run algorithm-based route scoring together with AI analysis of local and family conditions, and return fitness scores and plain-language recommendations across the full set of candidate heating and cooling pathways. On that basis, the same framework can help users compare ``which path fits this household and this place'' rather than assuming one universal technology.
